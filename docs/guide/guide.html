Reading the numbers

dB (decibels) compare two sounds. Speech vs noise and the voice you follow vs the others are level differences: at 0 dB the voice you follow is as loud as the rest; at −6 dB the rest is 6 dB louder. Lower is harder, so a falling threshold is progress. Echo time (RT60) is how long a room takes to fall 60 dB quiet. Points are percentage points. SD is a standard deviation; d and g are effect sizes in SD units. Sources appear as (author, year) and link to chapter 9.

Chapter 0101

What Earshot trains and why

Conversations break in three ways: other voices bury the one you want, distance and echo smear it, or the talker sounds unlike anyone you know. Earshot trains each of them, and the skill that ties them together: holding on to what you heard long enough to use it.

1. Other voices bury the one you want

Two kinds of masking

Energetic masking: the noise covers the sound, so parts of it never reach the ear in usable form.

Informational masking: the sound arrives, but its words get mixed up with the words around it. In multi-talker rooms this is the bigger problem (Bronkhorst 2015).

In a busy bar the voice you want is rarely too quiet to hear. More often it is lost in confusion: its words reach your ears and get mixed up with the words of the voices around it. In a classic task where listeners followed one talker among others, most errors were the other talker's words, and a competing voice of the same sex cost about 20 percentage points compared with one of the other sex; a near-identical voice cost about 40 (Brungart 2001, reported by Bronkhorst 2015). Two competing talkers are the hardest number: one talker leaves gaps to listen through, while two fill the gaps and still sound like words (Rosen et al. 2013). At a lively party, the voice you want is estimated to be about as loud as everything else around it (Plomp 1977, reported by Bronkhorst 2015).

Three cues pull a voice out of the crowd: knowing which voice to follow, knowing where it is, and how different it sounds. Knowing where to listen was worth 10 to 40 points in one study (Kidd et al. 2005, reported by Bronkhorst 2015). Small separations go a long way: 10 degrees between voices was enough for top scores in one task (Brungart & Simpson 2007, reported by the same review), and two talkers 15 degrees to each side of the voice you follow became 12 to 18 dB easier to ignore than when all three came from one place (Swaminathan et al. 2015). People also differ a lot: in that study, the level at which adults with normal hearing could follow one voice between two others spanned about 15 dB. Crowd, Seek and Voices train the use of these cues; Repair trains what to do with the gaps.

2. Distance and echo smear it

In the open, every doubling of distance takes about 6 dB off a voice. In a room the echo stays while the direct sound fades, so the share of direct sound falls with every step back (as summarised in the introduction of a 2024 study of distance perception, arXiv 2408.14198). Echo also blurs the end of one sound into the start of the next: in two very reverberant halls, listeners identified final consonants 56 and 62 % of the time, against 88 % without echo (Vlahou et al. 2021).

Ears adapt quickly. Hearing a sentence in the same room before the target improved the speech threshold by 2.7 dB, within seconds, mainly through both ears working together (Brandewie & Zahorik 2010). And in a game-like study that taught a new speech-sound contrast, training across three rooms carried over to rooms the listeners had never heard, while training in a single room produced no learning at all (Vlahou, Seitz & Kopčo 2019). Far is built on both findings.

3. The talker sounds unfamiliar

An accent, a fast talker, a whisper, a bad phone line: the first sentences are hard, then the ear tunes in. Across eight kinds of distorted speech, listeners made half their gain within about three sentences, with no feedback at all (Cooke, Scharenborg & Meyer 2022), and the slowdown caused by an unfamiliar accent was gone within about twelve sentences (Xie et al. 2018).

The open question is how to make the tuning-in carry over to the next new voice. Listeners who heard five talkers with five different accents understood a new talker with yet another accent better, while listeners trained on one accent did not (Baese-Berk, Bradlow & Wright 2013, ten listeners per group). A larger study found that kind of carry-over real but uneven from talker to talker, and found that a talker understood only about half the time produced no adaptation at all (Bradlow, Bassard & Paller 2023). Strange voices bets on variety, dosed so that the ear can keep up.

4. Holding on to what you heard

Catching a name in a noisy room is half the job; keeping it until you can use it is the other half. Attention and general thinking skills go with better listening among competing talkers, most clearly in older listeners (Füllgrabe, Moore & Stone 2015), and words that come later can repair a word the noise blurred earlier (Tinnemore, Gordon-Salant & Goupell 2025). Echo trains listening under memory load on the content that matters day to day: names, numbers, places and times.

5. What earlier trainers taught us

Listening trainers have been studied for three decades. People reliably got better at the exercises they practised (Henshaw & Ferguson 2013). Carry-over to untrained listening tests was smaller and less consistent, and it shrank as study quality rose: in a meta-analysis of 23 controlled studies, the randomised trials showed no clear effect on speech-in-noise thresholds (Fallahnezhad, Pourbakht & Toufan 2023), and the largest independent trial, with 279 hearing-aid users, found a commercial programme no better than a placebo (Saunders et al. 2016, abstract only). The few controlled successes were small and faded: an audiomotor game for older hearing-aid users (Whitton et al. 2017, known to us from a news release) and a game of listening tasks for young adults, whose gain was gone a month later (Lelo de Larrea-Mancera et al. 2022).

We read these studies as a map of how trainers fail, and built Earshot around the failures.

Chapter 0202

Getting started

Earshot runs in any modern browser, on a phone or a computer, online or off. A first-time player is training within two minutes. Three settings are worth one more minute each.

1. Open it

Earshot is a single web page. Open the web version (the project's GitHub Pages site), or download the file earshot.html and open it from your device's files. Neither needs an account. The downloaded file works without a connection from the start; the web version loads from its site and now and then checks it for a new version. Neither sends anything about you: your record stays in the browser you use, on that device.

The web version can be installed: use your browser's Install or Add to Home screen, and Earshot opens like an app and works offline after the first visit. A new version arrives whole, offered with a Reload button between sets, never during one. The downloaded file never installs anything; it simply runs from disk. Opened inside another app's built-in browser (Discord, Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, Snapchat, LINE or X), Earshot shows a notice until you dismiss it on that device: such browsers can lose saved progress, so open it in your browser instead.

The first time you open it, a short welcome tells you that Earshot was built by the Chimera community on Discord and invites you to join us at discord.gg/chmr; later launches show a one-tap version.

2. The first two minutes

Tap Start session. Each exercise opens with a short tutorial the first time you meet it: one line at a time that always says what to do next, a worked example with the right answer marked and every wrong option labelled with the kind of mistake it represents, then a few practice trials with feedback. A wrong practice answer gets a fresh try on a new trial, and a practice sound that cannot be prepared gets two tries before the tutorial moves on. Practice never counts toward your levels, and a Skip button is always there. The tutorial ends with the Discord link, and then the set begins.

To try one exercise on its own, open Home › Exercises. It lists all eight with a line on what each trains and the bet it makes: Play a set runs one set of it in your current mode, with its tutorial first if you have not played it yet, and How it works opens its Help.

The ? button on every trial opens the exercise's Help: the goal, every dial with its real range, tips, and the bet the exercise makes. Pause is always one tap away, and ending a set early costs nothing. Trials fit a phone held sideways without scrolling, and with a screen reader each trial has its own heading and the app announces when to answer, whether you were right, and the feedback.

3. Phone speaker or headphones

Everything in Earshot works on a phone speaker except the dials that place voices around you in space: Crowd's spatial separation and Seek's head-turn view. Those need headphones, because each ear must get its own signal.

Settings › Sound › Headphone check plays six rounds of three low hums and asks which one is quietest. Over speakers, one of the louder hums partly cancels itself in the air and seems quietest, which gives speakers away; five right out of six unlocks the direction dials, and guessing passes only about 2 % of the time (the anti-phase method of Woods et al. 2017). The check is a strong hint rather than proof: in its authors' tests about 1 in 20 desktop speakers passed, and more laptop speakers did. If you know you wear headphones, switch on I am wearing headphones instead.

4. A comfortable level

Settings › Sound › Set level plays a reference sound. Set your device volume so that it sounds clear and comfortable, like a conversation across a table, and never loud. Every trial except Seek's live search then plays at that same loudness, however hard it gets: difficulty changes the balance between the voice you follow and everything else, never the overall volume.

5. Safe listening

The WHO–ITU safe-listening standard gives adults a weekly sound allowance equal to 80 dB(A) for 40 hours, and every 3 dB louder halves the safe time: 83 dB(A) for 20 hours uses the same allowance (WHO–ITU 2019, ITU-T H.870).

Earshot's sessions are 10 to 30 minutes long and play at the level you chose, so a comfortable setting keeps you far inside that allowance. Turn the volume down if anything ever sounds loud, and give your ears a rest if they ring or feel dull after listening anywhere.

6. Before a Lab check-in

Lab check-ins compare you with yourself, week by week, so keep the conditions steady: a quiet spot, the same device, the same headphones or speaker, the same volume. Levels measured in steady noise hold up well on people's own devices: a matrix sentence test taken at home on consumer gear agreed with the lab within about 0.3 dB, with no effect of headphone type (Schwarz et al. 2026). Background noise in your room, though, is a masker the app cannot hear.

How to train

Tap Start and Earshot does the planning: which exercises, which voices and rooms, and how hard, trial by trial. This chapter shows what happens under the hood, so you know why a session looks the way it does and when to change the defaults.

A session lasts about 15 minutes; Settings offers 10, 15, 20 or 30. It is made of sets of about three minutes, each from a different exercise, so a 15-minute session holds five sets. The plan draws each set's exercise with weights that lean toward loud places (Crowd 20, Repair 12 and Seek 8 out of 100), then distance (Far 22), unusual voices (Strange voices 18), memory (Echo 12) and voice learning (Voices 8). An exercise does not come back until the others have had their turn, and never twice in a row. In your first sessions, up to three exercises you have not played yet open each session, so in Edge, Path and Variety you meet all seven by your third session. A set whose sounds cannot be prepared on your device, that is interrupted too often, or that you end before the first answer is passed over with the reason on screen, and the session goes on. Only sessions with at least one answered trial are logged and counted.

After some Strange voices, Far and Repair sets comes a listen-only interlude of about 25 seconds: examples of the condition you just trained, with their words on screen and nothing to answer, at most two per session. In lab studies, practice combined with periods of listen-only exposure learned as much as longer practice (Wright et al. 2010, with tones, and 2015, with speech; both known to us from their abstracts). There is never a repeat-aloud step: saying a sound right after hearing it blocked its learning in one study, while a delay of a few seconds restored it (Baese-Berk & Samuel 2022).

2. At the edge, on every dial

Each exercise has several dials: how loud the other voices are, how many there are, how alike they sound, how far away the talker stands, how much echo the room has. For every dial Earshot keeps its own estimate of your edge, the level at which you get about 7 in 10 right. On each trial it pushes one dial, the focus, to your edge and sets the others a margin below theirs, so every dimension advances and no single number hides the rest.

The trial mix

Edge trials aim at about 72 % right.

Anchors, about 90 %: every fourth or fifth trial, and after two misses in a row.

Warm-ups, about 85 %: the first trials of a set and of any new condition. They do not move your levels.

Floor: no trial is planned below 65 %.

Why about 7 in 10? Learning theory puts the fastest learning at about 85 % correct for simple learning machines (Wilson et al. 2019), while data from a learning platform found more learning and more returning players with harder targets of 50 to 65 % (Papoušek, Stanislav & Pelánek 2016). Earshot's edge sits between the two, and the easy trials around it pull the average up.

Which dial gets the focus depends on where you have been improving lately, with one trial in five chosen at random so that no dial is forgotten, a method from intelligent tutoring systems (Clement et al. 2015). Easy anchor trials are not wasted time: in visual learning studies, mixing easy trials with hard ones let learning happen even without feedback, and training only at threshold made learning more specific to the trained condition (Liu, Lu & Dosher 2012; Hung & Seitz 2014). Warm-ups exist because the ear needs a few sentences to tune in to a new voice or distortion (Cooke et al. 2022). And the floor exists because exposure that hard starves learning: a talker understood about half the time produced no adaptation (Bradlow et al. 2023; also McLaughlin et al. 2024).

3. Feedback that teaches

Every answer gets feedback at once, slot by slot: what was said, what you picked, and why a wrong option was tempting (a word another voice said, a sound-alike, a detail from elsewhere in the message). Then you can hear it again. In Crowd: the voice alone, then the crowd again. In Strange voices: the words appear and the same strange voice plays again. In Repair: the whole sentence, then the broken one with its words. In Far: the talker up close, then from the distance again.

The common ingredient is hearing the hard version again while knowing what it says. Distorted speech heard again after seeing its words trained listeners as well as hearing a clear version, and better than plain repetition (Davis et al. 2005); the degraded sound with text beat clear-sound feedback in another study (Loebach, Pisoni & Svirsky 2010), and interrupted-speech training with the same clear-then-degraded feedback carried over across gap types (Benard & Başkent 2013). Feedback never shows a running score; your progress appears between sets, in dB and metres.

4. Variety, dosed

Variety of talkers, rooms, noises and voice types is a dial of its own. It widens when you get at least 75 % of your last 20 edge trials right and narrows when that falls below 55 %, so new kinds of voice arrive one or two at a time and mixing grows as your accuracy holds.

The research supports both halves of that rule. Variety slows early learning and widens what the learning applies to (Raviv, Lupyan & Green 2022). Mixing talkers helped strong learners and hurt weaker ones in one study, where blocking rescued them (Perrachione et al. 2011), though a large replication found no such split (Brekelmans et al. 2022); a hard new speech contrast was learned better blocked than interleaved (Fuhrmeister & Myers 2020), while interleaving accented talkers beat blocking once accuracy was high (Tzeng et al. 2016). Block to acquire, mix to generalise.

5. Five modes, one record

Edge is the default and needs no setup. The other modes change how the next trial is chosen, never your record: switching back and forth loses nothing.

6. Sleep, days off and coming back

The first set of the first session on a new day re-tests the hardest condition you reached on your last training day, with a voice that was not in that set. In one study, listeners who learned an accent generalised it to a new talker only after a night's sleep (Xie, Earle & Myers 2018), so this set lets you see what the night did.

After two or more days away, a gentle fresh ears set comes first instead, and the app tells you whether your level held. In large data sets from online training games, skill mostly survived breaks and came back within a game or two (Kumar et al. 2022). After three sessions in one day, the plan suggests coming back after sleep: repeated same-day practice wore performance down in a visual task, and a nap restored it (Mednick et al. 2002). Nothing is lost by stopping.

The ring on the home screen counts this week's sessions. Four or five a week is a good rhythm, any day works, and a new week simply starts a new ring.

7. How much, and for how long

Four or five 15-minute sessions add up to about an hour a week. Controlled studies that found carry-over trained for roughly 12 to 40 hours in total (Table 1), and the total amount of practice mattered more than how it was spread over the week (Nogaki, Fu & Galvin 2007). So think in months: ten weeks at this rhythm is about 10 to 12 hours.

Gains from two game-based programmes faded within a month once practice stopped (Table 1), while interrupted-speech training held for about three months (Benard & Başkent 2013). Nobody has tested a maintenance dose, so ours is a bet: once your Lab trend levels off, keep one or two sessions a week, and let the retention check after a long break show what stayed.

8. Plateaus, and what the app changes

Individual learning curves rarely bend smoothly. In data from about 25,000 people on an online training site, most individual curves were better described by steps than by a smooth curve (Donner & Hardy 2015, known to us from a summary), and in a long study of a complex game, dips while players tried a new strategy often came just before a leap (Destefano & Gray 2016). A flat stretch is normal. A long one is a signal to change something.

Earshot watches each exercise for a stall: no new personal best in four sessions, and a trend over your last 10 sessions with that exercise whose upper 80 % bound stays below +0.15 dB a session, four checks in a row. The earliest it can say so is your 13th session with an exercise. In our simulations of learners over 60 days, the rule caught about half to more than four in five true stalls, depending on the mode, and gave a false alarm to 3 to 8 % of simulated learners who were in fact still improving slowly.

When it finds a stall, it changes one thing, tells you what and why, and waits at least three sessions before changing anything else. The changes come in this order:

  1. Wider variety: more voices, rooms and noises.
  2. A different focus: another dial is pushed to its edge for a while, the rest at comfort.
  3. More anchors: more easy trials between the hard ones.
  4. A strategy card with one concrete tip for that exercise.
  5. Sleep on it: when this is already your second session or more that day.
  6. A rest: the exercise sits out three sessions while the plan trains other situations, then comes back.

A plateau screen is also where Earshot invites you to the Chimera community on Discord; it never interrupts a set.

Chapter 0404

The eight exercises

Seven exercises train in the app, one takes training into your week. Each page says what you do, lists the dials with their real ranges from easy to hard, names the bet the exercise makes about carry-over, and sums up the research behind it.

All seven training exercises share one trial shape: a short cue (a call sign, a voice, a room), one sound played once, a closed set of answers to tap, and feedback with replays. Answer options are shuffled every time, and the wrong options are drawn from words that could have been the answer with the voices you are training on, so no option gives itself away: words another voice said, sound-alikes, and details from elsewhere in the message. Every sound is rendered on your device for that trial, so the same words come back with different voices, rooms and crowds.

Each exercise also lists its dials in its Help sheet (the ? on every trial), with the same ranges as below. "Easy to hard" runs from the first level to the last; the engine moves along each ladder as far as your edge allows.

Exercise 1 · loud places

Crowd

bet: attention and cues

Follow one voice while others talk over it. The crowd gets louder, more alike and closer together as you improve.

What you do

One voice says a call sign, then a word and a number. Other voices say words and numbers too, over a background of chatter. Follow the call-sign voice and tap its word and its number. As your variety widens, the cue that tells you whom to follow changes (a call sign, a short preview of the voice, or its place around you) and the voices start at slightly different moments.

When you pick a word another voice said, the feedback says so: in crowds that is the most common mistake, a slip in which voice you followed rather than in your ears. Then you can hear the voice alone, and the crowd again.

The bet

Attention control and voice and place cues against competing talkers; ever-changing crowds aim to keep the skill general.

The research

Speech among other speech is limited mostly by confusion, not audibility, and the confusion grows with similarity: same-sex and near-identical voices are far harder than a voice of the other sex (chapter 1). The cues that undo it are what Crowd trains. Knowing where the voice will be was worth 10 to 40 points (Kidd et al. 2005, reported by Bronkhorst 2015); performance kept improving while the voice stayed in one place (Best et al. 2008, 2010, reported by the same review), and recall fell by about 10 points when it moved between turns (Lin & Carlile 2015); and over headphones, generic 3D sound gave the same spatial benefit as each listener's own ear shapes did (Zenke & Rosen 2022).

The catch: in a training study, listeners who trained in two-talker babble beat those who trained in steady noise, but the gain stuck to the trained babble's language and was larger for the trained talkers (Van Engen 2012). Crowd therefore keeps changing the crowd: how many, how alike, where, and what sits underneath. Whether that variety builds a general resistance is untested; the Lab's Two voices check-in, with recorded voices you never train with, is where it would show.

Tips

  • Lock on at the call sign, then stay with that voice's pitch and pace.
  • Wrong voice, not wrong ears: a real word from another voice means you followed the wrong talker; replay the voice alone.
  • With headphones, listen toward the red dot. Even 10 to 15 degrees apart, place is one of the strongest cues.

The research

Listeners calibrate to a room fast. Tips

  • Let the room tune you in: hear the first phrase as a room, not a message.
  • Catch the start of each word, where echo blurs least; let the phrase fill in the ends.
  • In real rooms, close the distance: every halving gives the direct sound 6 dB more against the echo.

Tips

  • Wait for the end of the sentence before you choose.
  • Let the noise do the filling: with noise in the gap, trust what you seem to hear.
  • Listen through the gaps: chopped speech keeps its rhythm and gives glimpses of most words.

  • Tag each detail with its role as you hear it, so it does not end up answering the wrong question.

  • Hold on to the preview voice and pass the decoys by.
  • Point one ear at the voice (headphones): about 30 degrees, not face-on.

Chapter 0505

The Lab: measuring what carries over

Getting better at the exercises is the expected part. The Lab asks the question that matters: does it carry over to voices, rooms and formats you never trained on? Short check-ins, a baseline that absorbs practice, a weekly trend with its margin, and a verdict in plain words.

1. Close to training, and further away

Transfer research grades outcomes by their distance from training: near is an untrained test close to the trained task, far is a different kind of task or an outcome in daily life (research note R4). Runs are drawn as alternate forms (new combinations of items, voices and order), so you do not meet one fixed list every week.

Practice is the main trap in measuring change: on a matrix sentence test, scores improve by 1.1 to 1.2 dB from the first list to the second through practice alone, and by another 0.3 to 0.5 dB by the third (Puglisi et al. 2015). So every check-in's first run is a practice run that never counts.

After your first training session, the Lab offers one short block a day, five blocks in all. Matrix, Digits and Two voices get a practice run and two measured runs; Rooms, New voice and Story get a practice run and one measured run; the Diary needs no practice. Training never waits for the Lab: the check-ins are offered, not required.

3. Weekly check-ins

After the baseline, a check-in of about 8 to 9 minutes is offered once at least six and a half days have passed since the last one: Matrix and Two voices every week, one rotating check-in (Digits, then Rooms, New voice and Story in turn) and the Diary. While your Matrix verdict is not yet clear, for at most your first 8 check-ins, the check-in holds a second Matrix run (about 3 extra minutes), because two runs make each check-in steadier: in our simulations a true 1.5 dB gain read "clear gain" by the fourth check-in about 8 times in 10 with two runs, against about 6 in 10 with one. After 14 days or more away, the next check-in is a retention check: what stayed while you were gone.

4. Reading your trend

Every run gives an estimate with its own uncertainty, shown as “give or take”. The trend model fits all your runs at once: a starting level, a change per week, and a term that absorbs early practice, with test noise allowed for. It reports your gain over your baseline with its likely range (where the true change lies, 90 % sure) and one of four verdicts:

  • A clear gain: the gain reaches the smallest detectable change, and the likely range lies wholly above zero.
  • Likely a gain: the chance that you improved at all is 90 % or more.
  • Not yet clear: neither, so far. Keep going; more runs narrow the margin.
  • Below your baseline: a decline at least as large as the smallest detectable change, with the likely range wholly below zero.

How to read the card

  • Headline: the verdict, then your gain over the baseline with its likely range. A positive gain is better, whatever the unit.
  • The paragraph: how large a change must be to count as clear with your runs so far, and the chance that you improved at all.
  • The chart: one dot per run with its uncertainty, with better upward. The hollow dot is the practice run, the grey line your baseline, the blue line and band the trend and its likely range.
  • Below the chart (not shown): what the check-in measures, its run-to-run noise, and a table of every run.

No verdict appears before a measured run at least 5 days after your baseline. The smallest detectable change is 1.96 times the uncertainty of the gain; for one run before and one after, that is the classic 2.77 times the run-to-run SD, and averaging k runs on each side divides it by the square root of k (Blampied 2022; the arithmetic is in research note R4). For the Matrix check-in, 2.77 × 0.6 dB is about 1.7 dB with single runs and about 1.2 dB with two runs on each side.

That is the reason for the whole design. Controlled effects of listening training have been between about 0.3 and 1.3 dB (research note R1), smaller than the change one short clinical test can detect in one person. Averaged runs, alternate forms and a fitted trend are how a real gain of 1 to 1.5 dB can become visible in weeks rather than never.

5. What the numbers are, and what they are not

Lab numbers compare you with yourself. They are training measures on Earshot's own materials, taken on your own device at your own volume: not clinical measures, not normed against a population, and not a hearing test. A Matrix threshold of −6 dB means little on its own; a fall from your baseline with a clear verdict means your listening with these materials improved. Focus mode compares check-ins with our starting guesses to pick your weakest situation; the app says so, because the guesses are ours, not norms, and no norms exist for these materials.

Keep the conditions steady from week to week (chapter 2). Thresholds in steady noise tolerate an unknown volume well, but with fluctuating sounds they shift with absolute level, by about 4 dB between 55 and 80 dB SPL in published data (research note R4).

6. When to see an audiologist

Earshot cannot tell whether you have a hearing loss. See an audiologist or a doctor if you notice a change in your hearing, ringing in your ears or pain, or if following conversations is a struggle more often than not: a hearing test can show whether something other than practice would help. A sudden change in hearing, in one ear or both, needs prompt medical attention.

Earshot keeps the game where it helps learning: a night out to explore, a cast of voices to get to know, growth you can see in real units, and a weekly challenge to share. It leaves out what research found to hurt: streaks, lives and a running score during trials.

2. The cast

Earshot's training voices are a cast of named characters, each with a role at one of the venues. A cast card fills in when you meet its voice, and it turns "known" once you have picked that voice out by ear three times in Voices. Synthetic voices are labelled as synthetic.

3. Growth in real units

Results come between sets, never during them, and in units that mean something. The headline counts what you got right: where each trial asks for two or more answers, it counts answers, the line below counts trials, and a partly right trial gets a half-shaded mark in the row. After a set, a dial that moved at least one step harder, to a level you were tested at, gets a line with its old and new level (Distance, from 4 m to 6 m), and personal bests are kept per dial, in dB or metres. Milestones mark abilities, never time spent: "Equal footing" when you follow a voice as loud as the others (0 dB), "Through the crowd" at −6 dB. Each needs at least six trials of that dial, and like the map it reads the careful side of the estimate, at a level you were actually tested at. A set's results show at most two milestones; any others wait in Progress. You see the milestones you have reached and only the next one for each dial, never a wall of locked badges, and a personal best or a milestone is a natural moment for an invitation to the Discord community, never more than one card on a results screen.

4. The weekly night out

Each calendar week has one seeded challenge that everyone on the same version of Earshot plays identically: four stops (Crowd, Far, Strange voices and Repair) in a seeded order, each at a seeded venue, with six trials per stop on a fixed ladder from easy to hard. The first complete run of the week makes your card, spoiler-free: a tick or a cross per trial and the furthest level per stop, always with the link to discord.gg/chmr. Later runs that week are practice. Weekly trials never change your training levels.

There is no leaderboard. In a meta-analysis of gamified learning, competition on its own did not reliably improve outcomes, while competition combined with collaboration did (Sailer & Homner 2020), and points, levels and leaderboards raised the amount people did but not its quality (Mekler et al. 2017). The card shows your own night, to share and compare if you like.

5. Why there are no streaks

Many apps count consecutive days and warn you before a streak breaks. Earshot never does. Across seven studies, people whose logged streak was broken engaged less afterwards than people whose streak stayed intact, whatever they had actually done, and more so when they blamed themselves for the break (Silverman & Barasch 2023, from the abstract and press reports). A single missed day did not materially affect how a habit formed (Lally et al. 2010), and among 61,293 gym members, the best of 53 interventions was a tiny bonus for coming back after a missed workout (Milkman et al. 2021).

So the weekly ring starts again each week without comment, missed days cost nothing, and a return after a break is greeted with a fresh-ears set that shows what you kept, not a warning about what you missed.

6. Why there is no score during trials

During a set you see progress dots, never a score, lives or a countdown. In a children's working-memory game, an always-visible score cut training gains to less than a third (a slope of 0.10 against 0.35 without points), and a visible lives bar also hurt (Katz et al. 2014). Sound effects during trials slowed responses by capturing attention (Miranda & Palmer 2014, reported by Lumsden et al. 2016), which for a listening trainer is reason enough to keep every timed sound clean: nothing decorative moves or plays while a sound is on.

Feedback itself is good for motivation when it informs. In a meta-analysis of 128 experiments, positive, informational feedback raised people's later free-choice interest in a task, while controlling feedback and rewards that depend on performance lowered it, most of all rewards that not everyone can earn (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, read through their 2001 review). Earshot's feedback tells you what happened and what to listen for, and its milestones are abilities that anyone can reach at their own pace.

Chapter 0707

Your data and privacy

Everything Earshot knows about you stays in your browser, on your device. There is no account and no server, and the app sends your data nowhere.

1. On your device

Your learner record, training log, Lab runs, diary and game progress are saved in your browser's storage on the device you use. Earshot needs no connection once it is open, and every sound is rendered on the device. If the browser blocks storage (some private windows and previews do), the app says so plainly and suggests a backup before you close it. Saves carry a version number, so progress survives updates. Settings › Data › Delete everything removes your record from this browser after a confirmation; export a backup first if you might want it back.

2. Backups and moving devices

Settings › Data (or Lab › Export) makes a backup file of your whole record. Import on another device merges it in, older backup versions are upgraded, and nothing on the receiving device is deleted. A damaged file is refused whole, so a half-broken backup can never overwrite good data. Backups carry no name except a nickname you typed yourself, and no device details. Two spreadsheet exports (CSV) list your check-in runs and your training sessions. Backups, exports and the weekly card save as an ordinary download, or through the download prompt of a viewer that cannot download from its page; if nothing arrives, Copy puts the text on your clipboard.

3. The community export

To help test the bets, the Lab can write a pool file meant for sharing. It holds a random pooling id, days and weeks counted from your own start instead of dates, no record ids, and no absolute times except the date of the export, in UTC. It includes your nickname, and device, language or time-zone details, only if you tick them. Trial-level answers are included so that pooled files can calibrate how hard each item is. You decide whether and where to share it; the app never uploads anything.

4. Voices from your life

Recordings of people you know are stored only in this browser's database on this device, as trimmed 16 kHz mono audio, without the original file's name or metadata. Each needs a name and a consent tick. They are never included in backups or exports, so they do not move to another device either. You can delete any of them at any time.
