# R4 — Measuring speech-in-noise ability and related listening skills in a self-administered browser app

Research notes (evidence and methods only, no design decisions). Started 2026-11-26 15:20 UTC; last updated 2026-11-26 16:10 UTC. About 85 fetches and 37 searches; the shared web-search budget then ran out, so later work used only URLs already found (see Could not open, item 16).
Reading-depth tags: [full text] = read the paper or page itself; [abstract only]; [secondary report] = numbers taken from a review or another paper that reports them (named).

## Summary

1. Most precise per minute: closed-set matrix sentences or digit triplets in *steady* speech-shaped noise — matrix σ_w 0.4–0.6 dB with 20 sentences (~3–4 min); DIN σ_w ~0.7 dB young NH, SEM ~1 dB across ages with 24 triplets (~3 min). Slopes: matrix lists 12.6–17.1 %/dB, DIN 14.5–24.7 %/dB.
2. Cross-study median slopes (885 functions): steady noise 7.7, modulated noise 6.1, one competing talker 3.7, two talkers ~4.3 %/dB; ≥3–4 talkers approach steady noise. Shallower slope = less precision per trial.
3. Clinical babble tests are coarser and commercial: QuickSIN ±2.7 dB (1 list), ±1.9 (2), ±1.6 (3); WIN critical difference 3.1–3.5 dB; HINT ±2.41 dB per 10-sentence list. QuickSIN is a registered trademark; HINT is unobtainable; WIN uses VA recordings; matrix language versions are licensed products.
4. Practice effects are the main threat to "gain within weeks": matrix +1.1–1.2 dB list 1→2, +0.3–0.5 dB 2→3, with prior exposure still worth ~1.5 dB later. Stationary noise shows ~0.3 dB learning, modulated maskers 2.2–4.3 dB, babble ~2 dB over six runs. Two practice lists before baseline is the published norm.
5. Remote on one's own uncalibrated devices works for SNR thresholds in steady noise. Remote matrix test: ICC 0.80, RMSE 0.73 dB, bias vs lab 0.29 dB, no effect of headphone type or Bluetooth (n = 20 young NH). Online DIN at home: +0.52 dB vs lab (n.s.), mean absolute retest difference ~1.1–1.2 dB, between-person SD 1.44 vs 0.92 dB.
6. Fluctuating-masker SRTs depend on absolute level (~4 dB change between 55 and 80 dB SPL), unlike steady-noise SRTs. Without calibration this adds error to dip-listening probes.
7. Reliable change for one run vs one run = 2.77 × σ_w: ≈1.4 dB at σ_w 0.5, ≈1.9 dB at 0.7, ≈2.8 dB at 1.0. Averaging 4 runs per time point halves it. Absolute indices (σ_w, LoA, RMSE) matter more than ICC, which is low in homogeneous samples (online DIN ICC 0.09–0.33).
8. A Bayesian matrix procedure (BPACE) matched staircase SRTs with ~21 instead of 30 sentences (3.1 vs 4.8 min), lower rated effort (5.5 vs 7.3 of 10) and similar ICC.
9. Headphone checks: Huggins pitch at 6/6 passes 81% of real headphone users and 20% of loudspeaker users. Anti-phase at ≥5/6 lets 5% of desktop and ~18% of laptop speakers through; 64.7% of 5,154 online participants passed it. Combining checks cuts false passes to ~7% but rejects ~40% of real headphone users.
10. Timing is not trustworthy across devices. Browser audio–visual lags range up to ~286 ms with jitter up to ~40 ms SD; Bluetooth adds ~250–480 ms. Response-time effort measures (~150 ms per 3 dB SNR) are therefore within-device only.
11. Transfer distance as defined in the auditory-training literature: near = untrained outcome close to the trained task; far = different modality or construct. A talker ladder: same talker with new items, then a new talker with the same accent (needs multi-talker training), then a new accent (no transfer found). Phoneme training left steady-noise probes unchanged but moved competing-talker (d 0.47) and dual-task (d 0.77) probes, which are less reliable.
12. Self-report: the SSQ is stated to be in the public domain (SSQ12 paper). APHAB is free to download but copyrighted and may not be modified. HHIE authors ask for citation. NASEM 2025 found no MCIDs; APHAB-global critical difference ~16–18%. Retrospective ratings show response shift.
13. EMA: one week at 7–8 prompts/day captures most of a person's listening situations. Speech-in-noise moments are under-reported (~1/3 captured; ~2/3 with level-triggered prompts); people stop reporting in social settings.
14. Single-case standards: ≥3 demonstrations of effect, ≥3 (with reservations) or ≥5 points per phase, and ≥3 consecutive baseline probes in multiple-probe designs. Critics argue 3–4 stable points suffice and long baselines cause reactivity. Safe listening (WHO–ITU): 80 dB(A) × 40 h/week for adults, 75 dB(A) for children; dose doubles per +3 dB.
15. Gaps: Smits 2013, the DIN scoping review and the matrix multilingual review were not opened in full. The shared search budget ran out before real-world SNRs, hearWHO data, online-cohort reliability (Cooke 2021), reverberation/distance tests and CRM reliability could be searched.

## 1. Adaptive SRT procedures, slopes, reliability

### 1a. Digits-in-noise (DIN) / digit triplet test (DTT)

- **Origin and principle.** Digit triplets (e.g. "3-7-1") presented in a speech-spectrum noise, listener types the three digits; adaptive 1-up/1-down track on SNR; triplet scored correct only if all three digits are right; converges to 50% triplet-correct. Origin: Smits, Kapteyn & Houtgast 2004 (Dutch telephone test). Reviewed across ~39 papers by Van den Borre et al. 2021 (scoping review): steep psychometric functions, precise SRTs, strong correlation with pure-tone thresholds, large-scale use feasible. [abstract only — full text on KU Leuven Lirias could not be fetched; see Could not open]
- **Typical modern procedure (French antiphasic DIN, Ceccato et al. 2021)** [full text, via PMC]: 120-triplet bank; 200 ms gaps between digits; noise starts 500 ms before the first digit; start at 0 dB SNR; 1-up/1-down with 4 dB steps for the first 3 steps, then 2 dB; 23 triplets; SRT = mean of the last 19 SNRs. Digit levels equalised with 20 normal-hearing (NH) listeners at 10 fixed SNRs (−2 to −20 dB, 2 dB steps); per-digit level corrections were small (within ±0.4 dB). Antiphasic NH reference SRT −15.4 dB SNR (SD 1.3); diotic NH SRT about −10.7 dB (SD 1.3) in a small unpublished sample, i.e. ~5 dB binaural masking release from antiphasic presentation. No calibration in the app (any headphones). No test–retest data. Noise was regenerated every trial so the noise token cannot be learned. A national campaign logged 19,545 completed app tests in one month (2021).
- **Dutch DIN in children and adults (Koopmans, Goverts & Smits 2018, Ear Hear)** [full text, via PMC]: 120 triplets, male talker, LTASS noise; 150 ms gaps with ±50 ms random jitter; fixed overall level 65 dBA (SNR changed while keeping total level constant); 1-up/1-down, 2 dB steps; first triplet 6–8 dB above expected SRT; 24 triplets; SRT = mean SNR of trials 5–25 (the 25th is the level the next triplet would have had); ~3 min per condition. Measurement error (SEM) about 1 dB in all conditions/age groups (the paper cites 0.7 dB from Smits et al. 2013 for adults). Adult slopes at 50%: stationary diotic 14.5 %/dB; interrupted (fluctuating) diotic 10.6 %/dB (significantly shallower); antiphasic stationary 13.2; antiphasic fluctuating 12.8 %/dB. After one practice run, no significant learning except 0.7 dB (adults, diotic fluctuating) and 0.5 dB (adults, antiphasic stationary). Failure mode: some listeners heard a spurious "fourth digit" at noise onset; 2 children excluded for confusion.
- **Swahili DTT (Rosendahl, Tronstad & Øygarden 2025, S Afr J Commun Disord)** [full text, via PMC]: fixed-SNR development (not adaptive). Digit-level homogenisation by matching each digit to 80% point; mean shift 0.9 dB (SD 0.9), a few large outliers (up to 11.2 dB for one digit in last position). Slopes: digits 21.7 ± 5.4 %/dB (per subject), 20.0 ± 1.2 %/dB (per list); triplets 24.7 ± 3.5 %/dB (per subject), ~22–22.9 %/dB (per list; paper internally inconsistent). SRT triplet scoring −8.9 dB (SD 0.6 across subjects, 0.1 across lists); digit scoring −10.3 dB (SD 0.6 / 0.2). List equivalence by ANOVA, F(3,88)=0.66, p=0.58. A 20-triplet training list preceded data. Masker = the digit material overlaid 10,000 times with random delays (same long-term spectrum as the speech).
- **South African smartphone DIN in clinic (Potgieter, Swanepoel & Smits 2018, S Afr J Commun Disord)** [full text, via Europe PMC]: participants set a comfortable volume with a slider (no calibrated level); speech level fixed at positive SNRs, noise level fixed at negative SNRs; 2 dB steps; SRT vs best-ear four-frequency PTA r_s = 0.81 (n≈109–120); cut-off −9.5 dB SNR gave sensitivity 0.88 / specificity 0.88 (AUROC 0.94). No test–retest or slope reported. Authors disclose that the university assigned the app's IP for commercialisation (hearZA). Paper has several internal inconsistencies (step direction, sample sizes).

- **Smits, Goverts & Festen 2013 (JASA; volume/pages unverified)**: within-subject variability 0.70 dB for young NH adults; DIN–sentence-SRT correlation 0.96; steep psychometric function [secondary report: Araiza-Illan et al. arXiv 2312.12269; Koopmans et al. 2018]. **Contradiction between secondary reports on learning**: Potgieter et al. 2018 cite Smits 2013 for "no learning effect", Shehabi et al. cite it for training effects "up to ~1.5 dB at retest". Not resolved — the paper itself was not opened (see Could not open).

### 1a-2. Precision of adaptive sentence SRTs vs number of trials (simulation)

- **Dingemanse & Goedegebure 2020 (Trends Hear, article 2331216520919199)** [full text, PMC]: Monte-Carlo listener models fitted to real NH and CI data; word-scored stochastic-approximation tracks: SD of SRT ≈0.6 dB (NH) and ≈1.7 dB (CI users) for 26 sentences; a simulated matrix test gives test–retest SD 0.75 dB for 30 trials; 13 sentences gave high SD or bias for CI users — at least 20 sentences per estimate recommended; starting more than ~4 dB above the true SRT biases the estimate upward — start 2–4 dB below the expected SRT; within-track SD does not tell how accurate an individual estimate is; with SD 1.7 dB a 95% retest criterion is ~4.7 dB.

### 1b. Psychometric slopes by material and masker (cross-study)

- **MacPherson & Akeroyd 2014 (Trends Hear), systematic survey** [full text, Nottingham repository PDF; Table 2 cells partly garbled in the text layer]: 885 psychometric functions from 139 studies; slopes 0.4–43.8 %/dB; overall median 6.6 %/dB (mean 7.5). Median by masker (single masker): steady speech-shaped noise 7.7 %/dB; amplitude-modulated noise 6.1 %/dB; one speech masker 3.7 %/dB; two speech maskers ~4.3 %/dB; with 3–4 or more talkers slopes approach steady-noise values (5–20 maskers similar to 3–4). Digits in steady noise median 13.4 %/dB (IQR 6.1). Meaningful ("valid") sentences against a single speech masker can be as shallow as ~2.5 %/dB (garbled cell; verify). Hearing-impaired vs NH differences are small and confounded with age. Practical reading: near 50%, a 1 dB SNR change moves intelligibility by about the slope value in percentage points (logistic model; my inference, not the authors' recommendation).

### 1b-2. Clinical word and sentence tests in babble (US VA/Etymotic family) — numbers as reported

- **Wilson, McArdle & Smith 2007 (JSLHR 50:844–856)** [abstract only]: 24 NH, 72 hearing-loss listeners; BKB-SIN, QuickSIN and WIN use multi-talker babble with a descending fixed-SNR "method of constants"; HINT uses speech-spectrum noise with an adaptive track. 50% points: NH 1–4 dB signal-to-babble; hearing loss 5–14 dB. Group separation smallest for BKB-SIN and HINT (4–6 dB), largest for QuickSIN and WIN (8–10 dB). Authors judged QuickSIN and WIN the more sensitive measures.
- **WIN (Words-in-Noise; Wilson 2003; Wilson & McArdle 2007)** [secondary report: NASEM 2025 report, Appendix D and Ch. 6]: NU-6 monosyllables in babble fixed at 60 dB HL, words from 84 down to 60 dB HL in 4 dB steps (24 → 0 dB SNR), 5 words per step (35 words per list), Spearman-Kärber 50% point. Critical difference 3.1–3.5 dB (NASEM gives minimal detectable difference 3.5 dB); 0.3 dB shift across tests 12 months apart, ICC 0.88; ICC 0.89/0.91 over 40 days (mild/moderate loss); learning 0.4 dB across 4 runs (n.s.), <1 dB between runs; ~2–4 min per list (NASEM quotes 4–6 min for the full test).
- **QuickSIN (Killion et al. 2004, JASA 116:2395)** [secondary report: NASEM 2025]: IEEE sentences (5 key words) at 70 dB HL; four-talker babble rises so SNR falls 25 → 0 dB in 5 dB steps, 6 sentences per list; "SNR loss" from Tillman-Olsen/Spearman-Kärber formula (25.5 − words correct, relative to NH). ~1 min per list. 95% range for a single list ±2.7 dB; two lists ±1.9 dB; three lists ±1.6 dB. A different set of critical differences also quoted (3.9 dB one list per condition, 1.9 dB with four lists, 1.4 dB with eight). Test–retest r 0.92 (NH) / 0.97 (hearing loss) (Bentler 2000, as quoted). Learning <1 dB between runs. NASEM: moderate test–retest reliability, little evidence on consistency over time, English only.
- QuickSIN scoring details (Inventis product insight, Rev02, 2024) [full text]: 6 sentences/list, 5 key words each, 25 → 0 dB SNR in 5 dB steps, four-talker babble, SNR loss = 25.5 − total key words correct; NH needs about +2 dB SNR for 50%; categories 0–3 dB normal, 3–7 mild, 7–15 moderate, >15 severe; at least two lists per ear recommended; "available under license", product of Etymotic Research.
- **HINT (Nilsson, Soli & Sullivan 1994)** [secondary report: NASEM 2021 report on cochlear implants, Ch. 3]: 250 BKB-derived sentences in 25 phonemically matched 10-sentence lists, one male talker, steady speech-shaped noise, 1-up/1-down on speech level, sentence scored correct only if repeated entirely (variants allowing 1–3 word errors exist); ~2 min per list; 95% CI for a 10-sentence list ±2.41 dB in noise (±2.98 dB in quiet) from 18 NH listeners; heavy ceiling effects in quiet (28% of CI/HA users scored 100%); no longer available for purchase.
- **Procedural learning, older evidence (Theodoridis & Schoeny 1990, Audiology 29:228–239)** [abstract only, Scilit]: after one earlier 1-hour session (~250 words in noise), performance in a second session was better by the equivalent of ~1 dB SNR.
- **DIN meta-analysis (J Audiol Otol 2022;26:10–21)** [abstract only]: 14 studies; overall SMD 2.22 for hearing-loss detection; triplets gave lower (better) SRTs than single digits or pairs; speech-spectrum noise gave lower SRTs than multi-talker babble; native-language testing better than non-native.
- **NASEM 2025 consensus report "Measuring Meaningful Outcomes for Adult Hearing Health Interventions"** [full text of Ch. 6 and App. D, nationalacademies.org reader; secondary for the cited studies]: chose WIN as the strongest speech-in-noise outcome candidate (MDD 3.5 dB; in NIH Toolbox; ~4–6 min), QuickSIN a good candidate (±2.7 dB; two lists recommended). DIN not recommended as an outcome (used mainly for screening/diagnosis; insufficient evidence as an outcome measure; ~2 min). HINT excluded because it is not currently available for purchase for clinical use. Audible Contrast Threshold (ACT) judged too new. Notes that no MCIDs exist for QuickSIN, WIN, SSQ, APHAB-global, RHHI; and that one single MDC value across all baseline levels is not supported.

### 1c. Matrix (closed-set, syntactically fixed, semantically unpredictable) sentence tests

- **Principle (Hagerman 1982 — date from memory; OLSA staircase Wagener et al. 1999 as cited by Schmid et al. 2023; multilingual review Kollmeier et al. 2015)**: 50-word base matrix, 10 alternatives in each of 5 positions (name–verb–numeral–adjective–object or language-specific order), sentences recombined at random so they cannot be memorised; open-set (repeat) or closed-set (tap the 50-word panel); word scoring. Review covers 14 languages; optimisation yields steep functions and homogeneous lists; cross-language differences mostly from talker characteristics. [abstract only for the review]
- **Italian matrix test (Puglisi et al. 2015, Int J Audiol)** [full text, author postprint]: one female talker recorded 100 sentences covering all word-pair transitions (keeps co-articulation), cut into 500 word realisations; noise = 30-fold overlap of the speech material; word-level adjustments capped at ±3 dB; word-specific SRT SD fell from 3.4 (another manuscript copy says 3.7) to 1.4 dB after adjustment; predicted test slope rose from 9.7 (other copy: 9.2) to 15.2 %/dB. Authors: sentences are hard to memorise, so the test suits repeated measurement. Word-level median slope ~18 %/dB; list-level slope 13.3 ± 1.2 %/dB (range 11.1–15.6). Adaptive procedure per Brand & Kollmeier 2002 (word-scored, maximum-likelihood SRT), 20-sentence lists, noise fixed at 65 dB SPL, start 0 dB SNR. Reference SRT (after training): open-set −6.8 ± 0.8 dB, closed-set −7.3 ± 0.8 dB; list-level SRT range −7.6 to −7.2 dB (across-list SD 0.2 dB). Within-subject test–retest SD: 0.5 dB (open), 0.6 dB (closed). Learning: +1.2 dB (open) / +1.1 dB (closed) from list 1 to 2; +0.5 / +0.3 dB from list 2 to 3; small thereafter; recommendation = two 20-sentence practice lists. Cited cross-language values: slopes Spanish 13.2, French 14.0, Russian 13.8, Danish 12.6, German and Polish 17.1 %/dB; test–retest French 0.4 dB (closed), Russian 0.6/0.5 dB; across-list SD in other matrix tests 0.1–0.2 dB.

## 2. Procedural learning and alternate forms

### 2a. Size and time course of learning on repeated tests

- **Matrix tests**: +1.1–1.2 dB from list 1 to 2, +0.3–0.5 dB from list 2 to 3, small afterwards (Italian matrix, Puglisi et al. 2015) [full text]; developers recommend two 20-sentence practice lists before the measured list. People with earlier exposure remain ~1.5 dB better months later, and ~2 dB improvement over a year of repeated use is cited (Schwarz et al. 2026) [full text] — i.e. matrix-style closed sets carry slow, long-lived procedural/vocabulary learning (the 50-word set is learned).
- **DIN**: after one practice run, residual learning is ≤0.7 dB and mostly non-significant (Koopmans et al. 2018) [full text]; online DIN retest ~0.4 dB better despite a practice block (Shehabi et al.) [full text]; telephone DIN mean retest change 0.34 dB (Dillon et al. 2016, as cited by Shehabi et al.); Smits et al. 2013 is cited both for "no learning" and for "up to ~1.5 dB" (see 1a; unresolved) [secondary reports].
- **Masker modulation drives learning (Lie, Zekveld, Smits, Kramer & Versfeld 2024, JASA 156:341–349)** [full text, Amsterdam UMC repository]: Dutch everyday sentences, Plomp & Mimpen adaptive procedure, 13-sentence lists, 18 NH listeners, six lists per masker after an 18-sentence practice list. Exponential learning fits: stationary speech-shaped noise 0.3 dB (n.s.); 8 Hz interrupted noise 3.1–4.3 dB (duty cycles 50–65%); spectrally modulated noise 2.2 dB; half of the effect reached after ~0.5–3.6 lists (on average about two lists). Learning did not transfer between masker conditions and was not removed by prior practice in another condition. Interpretation: learning to "listen in the dips" (masking release) is a perceptual skill that improves with exposure — probes with modulated maskers will show large early gains even without training.
- **AzBio sentences, six runs (Buss, Calandruccio & Hall 2015, Ear Hear 36:e14–e22)** [full text, CORE PDF]: first-to-sixth-run threshold improvement ~0.9 dB in speech-shaped noise, ~2.0 dB in multi-talker babble, ~1.8 dB in AM noise (≈0.3 dB per run linear trend); attributed to procedural learning (citing Yund & Woods 2010).
- **WIN / QuickSIN**: <1 dB between runs; WIN 0.4 dB across 4 runs (n.s.) (NASEM 2025, secondary report).
- **Yund & Woods 2010 (Ear Hear; volume/pages unverified)**, "content and procedural learning in repeated sentence tests": cited by Buss et al. for procedural learning across repeated HINT/QuickSIN-type testing. [could not open; secondary report only]
- **PART CRM spatial task**: significant session-1 to session-2 improvement of ~1.1 dB in the separated condition (Lelo de Larrea-Mancera et al. 2020) [full text]; home version session difference −1.59 dB separated (2022) [full text].

### 2b. How published tests build equivalent alternate forms

- **Equalise item difficulty, then balance lists.** DIN: measure each digit's intelligibility function in NH listeners at fixed SNRs; shift each digit's level so all digits reach 50% (or 80%) at the same SNR; corrections typically within ±0.4 dB (French) to ~0.9 dB mean (Swahili, with outliers); reject recordings with poor fits (Ceccato et al. 2021; Rosendahl et al. 2025) [full text]. Matrix: per-word level corrections capped at ±3 dB to keep prosody natural; reduced word-SRT SD 3.4 → 1.4 dB; drop word tokens whose SRT deviates >8.5 dB; compose lists phonemically balanced; then verify list SRTs in a new NH sample (across-list SD 0.1–0.2 dB) (Puglisi et al. 2015) [full text].
- **Verification statistics used**: ANOVA across lists (Swahili DTT F(3,88)=0.66, p=0.58) [full text]; across-list SD of SRT (matrix 0.1–0.2 dB) [full text]; for QuickSIN, McArdle & Wilson 2006 (J Am Acad Audiol 17:157–167) evaluated homogeneity of the 18 lists [could not open — bot wall; known only that list homogeneity was tested].
- **Unlimited alternate forms by recombination.** Matrix sentences and digit triplets are drawn at random from a closed vocabulary, so a fresh "form" exists for every run and content (item) memory is impossible; only procedural/vocabulary learning remains (Kollmeier et al. 2015 review) [abstract only].
- **Noise tokens**: regenerate (or randomly offset) the masker each trial so the noise sample itself cannot be learned (Ceccato et al. 2021) [full text]; avoid audible loop seams (Zhao et al. 2022) [full text].

## 3. Masker choice for probes

- **Slopes by masker (MacPherson & Akeroyd 2014)** [full text]: steady speech-shaped noise median 7.7 %/dB; amplitude-modulated noise 6.1; one competing talker 3.7; two talkers ~4.3; 3–4+ talkers approach steady-noise slopes. Shallower slope = each dB of SRT is less certain for the same trial count, so precision per minute is best in steady noise and worst with one or two competing talkers.
- **Francart, van Wieringen & Wouters 2011 (Int J Audiol 50:2–13)** [abstract only]: seven maskers with Dutch materials (incl. ICRA-5 fluctuating noise, ISTS, competing talkers in Dutch and in Swedish). Slopes steepest for stationary maskers, then modulated noises, then competing talkers. NH listeners benefit from temporal gaps and from temporal fine structure; performance drops when the competing talker is in an understood language; ISTS carries a large informational-masking component. Recommendation: non-modulated maskers when measurement time matters; fluctuating noise to measure release from masking via gaps; a foreign-language competing talker to probe temporal-fine-structure use.
- **Talker number (Rosen, Souza, Ekelund & Majeed 2013, JASA)** [full text, UCL repository]: IEEE sentences, male target, all-male maskers of 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 talkers as natural babble, vocoded babble and speech-envelope-modulated noise, plus steady SSN. Natural babble was non-monotonic with talker number (hardest at 2 talkers, slight easing up to 16); modulated noise worsened up to a breakpoint of ~5 talkers then plateaued. Only a 1-talker speech masker and 1–2-talker modulated/vocoded maskers were easier than SSN. Authors' summary (paraphrased): the masker most similar to the target always masked most. Distributions of SRT overlapped across conditions only for 1- and 2-talker speech maskers (largest individual spread there). Procedure: Plomp & Mimpen-type up-down, masker 65 dB SPL, 4 → 2 dB steps, 20 sentences per SRT.
- **Green & Rosen (UCL, SPIN workshop talk, year not printed; c. 2014)** [full text, slides/abstract PDF]: N = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 talker babble, same- vs different-sex maskers, fixed SNRs, 8 groups × 10 NH. With same-sex maskers sentence recognition drops steeply from 1 to 2 talkers, while words and VCVs plateau only at larger N; with different-sex maskers materials behave more alike; breakpoints above N = 4 for different-sex maskers. Prior minima: N = 2 for IEEE sentences (Rosen et al. 2013); N = 8 for VCV consonants (Simpson & Cooke 2005, as cited). So "hardest babble size" depends on target material and talker-sex match.
- **Holube 2012 (ISAAR 2011 proceedings), German matrix test with many maskers** [full text]: median SRTs in NH — stationary OLSA noise about −7 to −8 dB; fluctuating ICRA5-250, ISTS and IFFM about −20 to −22 dB (≈13 dB masking release); interquartile ranges usually larger for fluctuating than stationary maskers. **Level dependence**: with the fluctuating IFFM masker, NH SRT improved from −15.6 dB at 55 dB SPL to −18.5 at 65 and −19.8 at 80 dB SPL (~4 dB over 25 dB) — dip listening depends on absolute audibility, so fluctuating-masker SRTs are *not* level-independent the way stationary-noise SRTs are. Closed-set responses lowered SRT by 0.6 dB (n.s.) in stationary noise but 1.2 dB (significant) with ISTS. Babble rows (2, 4, 8, 120 talkers) suggest 4–8-talker babble was harder than stationary noise for matrix sentences (table garbled; verify). Hearing-impaired listeners lost 2.7 dB vs young NH in stationary noise but up to 13 dB with one male competing talker.
- **Pedersen, El-Samaïl, Daugaard, Juhl & Andersen 2014 (ISAAR 2013 proceedings), Danish matrix test (Dantale II)** [full text]: four maskers (stationary Dantale II noise, ICRA-4, IFFM, two female talkers "2FS"); 19 NH retested after 20–62 days: test–retest variation did *not* differ across maskers (F(3,72)=0.46), contrary to expectation (numbers only in a figure). In 18 HI listeners the 2-talker masker SRT was the only one correlating significantly with an SSQ item (ρ = −0.54) — speech maskers may align better with self-reported difficulty.
- **Learning interacts with masker type**: stationary noise shows almost no practice effect (0.3 dB), modulated maskers 2–4 dB (Lie et al. 2024) [full text]; babble ~2 dB vs SSN ~0.9 dB over six runs (Buss et al. 2015) [full text]. DIN in interrupted noise also has a shallower slope (10.6 vs 14.5 %/dB) and a larger between-person SD after age correction (1.3 vs 0.8 dB in adults) than stationary DIN, while the reported measurement error was ~1 dB in all conditions (Koopmans et al. 2018) [full text].
- **What each masker measures (synthesis of the above)**: steady SSN = energetic masking, closest to "audibility in noise", most reliable, least learning; modulated noise = ability to use temporal dips (glimpsing), sensitive to hearing loss but shows big early learning; multi-talker babble (≥4–8 talkers) = mixture of energetic + some informational masking, closest to "party" ecology; 1–2 competing talkers = informational masking/selective attention/talker segregation, largest individual differences, shallowest slopes, least reliable per trial; foreign-language talker = fine-structure/segregation without lexical interference.

## 4. Near / mid / far transfer probes

### 4a. How the auditory-training literature defines distance

- **Ferguson & Henshaw (c. 2015), "How does auditory training work? Joined up thinking and listening" (Semin Hear; journal/year not printed on the repository copy)** [full text, Nottingham repository]: *on-task learning* = improvement on the trained task (necessary, not sufficient); *near transfer* = an untrained outcome close to the trained task; *far transfer* = an outcome in a different modality or measuring a different construct; "generalisation"/"off-task learning" used interchangeably. Outcome classes: speech perception (phonemes, words, sentences; energetic vs informational masking), cognition (working memory, attention, executive switching/monitoring), self-reported disability (e.g. GHABP), objective markers (pupil, alpha), adherence and retention. Key methodological observation: simple energetic-noise tests (digit triplets, sentences in modulated noise) did *not* change after phoneme training, whereas a competing-talker test (d = 0.47) and a dual-task listening/memory test at 0 dB SNR (d = 0.77) did; more complex tests tended to have lower test–retest ICC, so sensitivity and reliability must be traded off.
- **Henshaw & Ferguson 2013 (PLoS ONE 8:e62836), systematic review of computer-based auditory training** [full text, Nottingham repository]: 13 studies (from 229 screened), quality very low to moderate (none high). On-task learning significant in 9/10; untrained speech-in-noise improved in 11/13 but small and inconsistent (e.g. +6.9% open-set words; +14.9% words; LACE QuickSIN effect sizes 0.23–0.31, HINT n.s. with control group also improving); cognition 1/1 (Stroop, listening span after LACE); self-report 1/2 (HHIE/HHIA ~0.4 effect; SSQ no change). Only 2 studies repeated baseline testing to an asymptote; most did not separate test–retest/procedural learning; no blinding; median n = 9.5. Recommendations: practise lists before baseline, combine behavioural + self-report + cognitive outcomes, define clinically meaningful change, standardise outcomes.
- **Ferguson, Henshaw, Clark & Moore 2014 (Ear Hear 35:e110–e121), phoneme-discrimination RCT, n = 44, age 50–74** [full text, CORE PDF]: on-task gains on all 11 trained continua; untrained SRTs (digits, sentences) unchanged; complex attention (TEA dual-task decrement d = 0.53) and visual letter monitoring (d = 0.50) improved but simple digit span did not; GHABP disability improved (d = 0.51), "group conversation" item d = 0.68; SSQ unchanged; gains retained at 4 weeks.
- **Stropahl, Besser & Launer 2020 (Ear Hear 41:697–704), state-of-the-art review** [secondary report: ASHA Evidence Map summary]: outcome domains used — objective speech perception, self-reported communication, on-task learning, cognition (attention, processing speed, working memory), long-term retention, compliance. 10 studies found significant speech gains, 6 found no transfer; 3/4 that tested retention found benefits persisting 2 weeks–6 months; compliance 70–80%, with more compliant participants gaining more.

### 4b. A ready-made talker-distance ladder (adaptation to unusual talkers)

- **Bradlow & Bent 2008 (Cognition), Exp. 2** [full text, PMC author manuscript]: training on Mandarin-accented English over two sessions on consecutive days; tests in white noise at +5 dB SNR. Test talker types: (near) trained talker, new sentences; (mid) a *new* talker with the same accent; (far) a talker with a *different* accent (Slovak). Multi-talker training (5 talkers) and talker-specific training raised the new same-accent talker to ~90 RAU vs ~82 RAU after single-talker training or task-only practice and ~65 RAU untrained; single-talker training was no better than task practice; no training condition transferred beyond task practice to the different accent (~72 RAU for all trained groups vs 59 untrained). Talker-independent adaptation requires exposure to several talkers of the accent. Task practice alone gave large gains — controls must take the same probe tests.
- Baese-Berk, Bradlow & Wright 2013 (JASA EL) reported that training on several *different* accents generalised to a new accent [not opened — PMC copy behind bot check; claim from title/summary only].

### 4c. Candidate probe families as used in the cited literature

- *Near (same task family, untrained items/talkers/maskers)*: new sentences or digit strings from trained talkers; new talkers; new masker types (e.g. trained on babble, probe with competing talker); spatially separated maskers (PART SRM task).
- *Mid (different speech task or listening condition)*: words or sentences in competing talkers (informational masking) when training used energetic masking; dual-task listening + recall in noise (Ferguson & Henshaw); time-compressed, vocoded, accented speech for a new talker class; spatial release from masking.
- *Far (different construct or modality, real life)*: working memory and attention (TEA dual task, visual letter monitoring; simple span tasks were insensitive); Stroop; self-reported disability in group conversation; EMA of real listening situations; listening effort (response time, ratings).
- **Listening effort via response time**: Houben, van Doorn-Bierman & Dreschler 2013 (Int J Audiol) [abstract only]: with digit triplets in stationary noise, response time kept falling as SNR improved even where intelligibility was already near 100% (12 NH); an arithmetic variant (sum first + last digit) was more noise-sensitive but more variable. So RT can index effort at ceiling — but browser audio-onset timing varies by tens of ms across devices (Bridges et al. 2020), so RT probes are only comparable within a device.
- **Effort ratings**: BPACE showed rated effort 5.5 vs 7.3 (0–10) for higher-intelligibility vs 50%-tracking procedures (Schmid et al. 2023) [full text] — effort ratings are sensitive to the operating point of the test itself.
- **Verbal RT vs pupil vs rating (Visentin, Valzolgher, Pellegatti, Potente, Pavani & Prodi; approved manuscript, c. 2022)** [full text, Trento repository]: Italian matrix sentences in stationary noise at −3/−6/−9 dB SNR, 24 NH. Verbal RT (audio offset → response onset) was ~144–154 ms faster per 3 dB SNR step; 9-point effort ratings changed ~2.2–2.4 points per step; pupil dilation separated only the hardest SNR; RT and ratings correlated r = 0.76; pupil did not converge with them. 22% of pupil traces lost to blinks. Conclusion: verbal RT is a simple, SNR-sensitive effort index.
- **Digit-arithmetic RT in hearing loss (Reinten, de Ronde-Brons, van den Tillaart-Haverkate, Houben & Dreschler 2019, ISAAR proc. 7)** [full text]: 12 HI listeners; sum of first and third digit; RT strongly SNR-dependent (F = 107) and still falling where intelligibility was near maximal; no reliability statistic reported.
- **A lab speech-on-speech measure relates to self-report**: in 43 adults aged 40–69, a PART spatial-release-from-masking (speech-on-speech) task explained the most variance in HHIE-S scores, beyond age, audibility and cognition, more than the audiogram (Lelo de Larrea-Mancera et al. 2024, Am J Audiol 33:55–65) [abstract only].

## 5. Detecting an individual's real change within weeks

### 5a. Reliable change (RC/RCI)

- **Jacobson–Truax RCI, as restated by Blampied 2022 (Cogn Behav Ther 15:e50)** [full text, Cambridge PDF]: SEM = SD₁·√(1 − r_xx) (SD from a control or pre-treatment group; r_xx = reliability); S_diff = √2·SEM; standardised change = (X₂ − X₁)/S_diff; RCI(.05) = 1.96·S_diff in raw units; classify reliably improved / deteriorated / indeterminate. 1.645 (90%) is common in neuropsychology. Jacobson & Truax recommended short-interval test–retest reliability (ICC for rating scales), not internal consistency, though practice varies. Practice effects must be handled separately (Duff 2012 methods, from a constant control-group adjustment up to regression-based prediction). The proportion of individuals reliably improved can serve as a study-level effect size.
- **For adaptive SRTs the within-subject test–retest SD (σ_w) is the SEM directly**, without needing a between-subject SD or r. Then S_diff = √2·σ_w and a 95% reliable change for one test vs one test = 2.77·σ_w (e.g. σ_w 0.5 dB → 1.4 dB; 0.7 dB → 1.9 dB; 1.0 dB → 2.8 dB). Averaging k independent runs at each time point divides σ by √k. (Arithmetic from the definitions above; the input σ_w values are in the facts table.)
- **Mixed-model reliable change (Gavett, Ashendorf & Gurnani 2015, J Int Neuropsychol Soc 21:558–567)** [full text, eScholarship]: fit a linear mixed model with random intercepts and slopes over repeated visits; compare an observed follow-up score with the model-predicted score; 90% interval = residual SE × 1.645 (bootstrapped prediction variance; t quantile in small samples). Practice is modelled explicitly through visit number (e.g. one-point gain every ~2 visits on a story-memory test), and baseline is a predictor, which handles regression to the mean. They advise interpreting RC alongside base rates of change in healthy samples.

### 5b. Faster and more comfortable adaptive estimation

- **BPACE (Schmid, Wimmer & Kompis 2023, Trends Hear 27)** [full text, Bern repository]: Bayesian matrix-test procedure; logistic model with threshold, slope and lapse rate on discrete grids with normal priors (threshold prior mean 0 dB, grid −15 to +20 dB in 140 steps; slope prior mean 0.13 dB⁻¹ in the paper's own parameterisation, grid 0.05–0.22; lapse 0–0.4), posterior via the UML toolbox (Shen et al. 2015); picks the next SNR at "sweet points" that minimise expected variance, alternating like a 1-up/1-down between the threshold, upper-slope and lapse sweet points (never the low-intelligibility sweet point, to spare the listener); stops when the 95% credible interval of the SRT is <2 dB or at 30 sentences. 20 NH + 20 CI users: SRTs identical to the standard 30-sentence staircase (NH −6.6 dB both; method effect 0.01 dB); mean 21.5 sentences (SD 6.3), 3.1 vs 4.8 min; rated listening effort 5.5 vs 7.3 on 0–10; mean proportion correct ~60–63% instead of ~52–54%; test–retest ICC NH 0.79 (vs 0.70), CI 0.89 (vs 0.89); |retest difference| <1.1 dB in 33/40 pairs for both methods. Caveat: the credible interval ignores slope/lapse uncertainty; slope estimates are weaker.
- **Short tracks**: a single 20-trial track gives a "reasonably accurate" tone-in-noise threshold online; the first track is least stable (Zhao et al. 2022) [full text]. DIN reaches ~1 dB measurement error with 24 triplets in ~3 min (Koopmans et al. 2018) [full text].

### 5c. Single-case designs (how to show an effect within one person)

- **WWC single-case design standards (Kratochwill, Hitchcock, Horner, Levin, Odom, Rindskopf & Shadish 2010)** [full text, ERIC PDF]: the independent variable must be manipulated by the researcher; ≥3 attempts to demonstrate an effect at three different points in time (plain AB designs do not qualify); a phase needs ≥3 data points to count; multiple-baseline designs need ≥6 phases with ≥5 points per phase to "meet standards" (≥3 points: "with reservations"); ABAB needs 4 phases × ≥5 points. Visual analysis on level, trend, variability, immediacy (last 3 vs first 3 points of adjacent phases), overlap and consistency. Strong evidence = ≥3 demonstrations and no non-effects. Inter-assessor agreement requirements (≥20% of points) apply to human-rated outcomes, not to automated scores. The 2010 document does not specify multiple-probe rules (Horner & Baer 1978 introduced the multiple-probe variant: intermittent probes instead of continuous baseline measurement) [Horner & Baer not opened].
- **Later WWC multiple-probe rule and its critique (CEC Division for Research position statement, Harris, Stevenson & Kauffman, adopted Oct 2019)** [full text, PDF]: WWC requires ≥3 consecutive probe points for each case within the first three sessions and 5 points per phase for multiple baselines; the statement argues 3–4 stable points suffice when stability is shown, proposes 3 consecutive probes for the first case and, for later cases, 3 probes before intervention plus one probe concurrent with the previous case's probes; warns that long baselines can cause boredom, fatigue, declining performance and reactivity to assessment (which inflate apparent effects).
- Relevance as described in these sources: staggered introduction of training on different skills (or staggered start across people) with untrained probes measured throughout gives within-person replication; a probe that rises only when its matched skill is trained, while the others stay flat, separates training effects from test practice.

## 6. Browser practicalities

### 6a. Headphone screening tests (published methods, pass and false-pass rates)

- **Anti-phase (AP) loudness test (Woods, Siegel, Traer & McDermott 2017, Atten Percept Psychophys)** [full text, PMC]: three 200 Hz tones of 1000 ms (100 ms ramps, 500 ms gaps); one tone 6 dB quieter; one of the two louder tones is anti-phase between channels (0°/180°); task "which is quietest?"; 6 trials, no feedback, pass ≥5/6 (guessing probability of ≥5/6 = 0.0178). Online levels −6.5 / −12.5 dB re a calibration noise. Lab: 20/20 on headphones passed; 19/20 on desktop loudspeakers (±30°, 70 dB SPL) failed (≈5% false pass); on participants' own laptop speakers the screen missed 4/22 (≈18% false pass), 3/22 said they used surface vibration. KEMAR: anti-phase attenuation >20 dB at all loudspeaker positions. Online (MTurk, n = 5,154): 64.7% passed, bimodal scores (modes 0 and 6). Control version without anti-phase tones: 12% failed (baseline failure from inattention/hearing). Setups that sum channels to mono, subwoofer-only output, or poor low-frequency speakers interact with the test (the antiphase tone cancels). 3.6% of online participants reported hearing impairment.
- **Huggins-pitch (HP) test (Milne, Bianco, Poole, Zhao, Oxenham, Billig & Chait 2021, Behav Res Methods 53:1551–1562)** [full text, publisher page]: 3-interval forced choice, three 1000 ms white-noise bursts; two diotic, one carries a dichotic Huggins pitch (180° interaural phase shift in a ±6% band around 600 Hz) which is heard only over headphones. 6 trials drawn from 12 pre-generated; recommended pass = 6/6. "Trusted" lab-recruited sample (n=100) tested on both equipment types: HP 6/6 — headphones 81% pass, loudspeakers 20% pass; HP 5/6 — 85% / 30%; AP 6/6 — 70% / 31%; AP 5/6 — 86% / 42%; both tests combined — loudspeaker pass ~7% but ~40% of real headphone users rejected. AUC: HP 0.821 vs AP 0.736 (HP better, p≈0.02); combined 0.844; max d′ ≈1.7. Unknown online sample (Prolific, n=100): mixture modelling estimated ~40% were not on suitable headphones (or were in noise). A third test (binaural beats, "BT") combined with HP gave ~7% false-pass and ~60% hit rate. Authors judge HP alone sufficient unless dichotic/spatial manipulations are critical. Failure causes for true headphone users: inter-channel crosstalk, low-quality equipment, background noise, possibly hearing loss; 14 of the trusted participants quit early because of incompatible equipment.
- **BMLD chirp-direction check (Mok et al. 2024, Behav Res Methods 56:1433–1448)** [full text, publisher page]: second check, judge the direction of a 150–400 Hz chirp embedded in N0Sπ noise (audible only with binaural masking-level difference, i.e. separate ear channels). Used together with the AP test, 6 trials each, pass ≥5/6 (≥4/6 in a hearing-loss cohort). Authors state the checks are neither 100% sensitive nor specific; pass rates not reported.
- Zhao, Brown, Holt & Dick 2022 (Trends Hear 26) [full text, author PDF]: used HP (6 trials, 6/6 required, two attempts allowed); took ~3 min; 34% attrition overall (91 started, 60 analysed), part of it around the headphone check; pass rate not reported.

### 6b. Level handling without calibration (what others did)

- Smartphone DIN apps let users set a comfortable level with a slider and report SNR-based SRTs; no calibration (Potgieter et al. 2018; Ceccato et al. 2021) [full text]. Many DIN versions keep the *overall* level fixed (e.g. 65 dBA) and vary SNR by trading speech and noise level (Koopmans et al. 2018) [full text]; others fix speech at positive SNRs and fix noise at negative SNRs (Potgieter et al. 2018) — both keep the mixture loud enough and avoid clipping at very favourable or very poor SNRs.
- Mok et al. 2024 [full text]: participants set system volume to 10–20% of maximum, then adjusted a calibration sound to "clearly audible and comfortable"; stimuli capped at +6 dB above the calibration sound (protects against clipping and loud surprises).
- Zhao et al. 2022 [full text]: self-calibration of level with a documented reference-noise RMS; thresholds varied with device/environment (AirPods ~3 volume steps worse; outdoor vs anechoic tone-in-noise thresholds correlated r = 0.82 but 4.7 dB higher), so they recommend per-participant level setting; looped noise gave audible "hiccups" at loop points (seam artefacts); they advise Chrome on laptop/desktop, notifications off, no phones/tablets.
- PART lab validation (Lelo de Larrea-Mancera et al. 2020) [full text, eScholarship]: two headphone models whose outputs differed by ~14 dB (66 vs 80 dB SPL) produced no significant difference in threshold measures, i.e. many supra-threshold measures are robust to absolute level once audible.
- PART at home on participants' own phones/tablets (Lelo de Larrea-Mancera et al. 2022, JASA 152:807–819) [full text, PMC]: 134 students over 3 experiments; own devices (mostly iOS); volume set to maximum with an automated prompt if not; levels reported only as nominal app dB because true SPL unknown. Composite test–retest r 0.65 (lab) vs 0.67 (home), not different. Calibrated vs uncalibrated equipment: no significant effect (MANOVA) in within-subject comparisons. Home testing on own devices produced more ±3 SD outliers (e.g. 12 on temporal modulation among 40) and mean scores somewhat worse than older lab norms (d ≈ 0.4–0.6), attributed partly to cohort/pandemic timing. 4/40 excluded for failing an audibility criterion. A video call was used to check left/right channels in one experiment.

### 6c. Why SNR-based measures tolerate unknown volume (and where they stop)

- An SNR-tracked SRT depends on the target-to-masker ratio, not the absolute level, as long as both are well above threshold and below distortion: supported indirectly by the absence of level/equipment effects in PART (14 dB output difference; calibrated vs uncalibrated) [full text] and the remote matrix test (no headphone-type effect, remote vs lab bias 0.29 dB) [full text], and by smartphone DIN apps run at self-set comfortable levels with high correlations to audiometry [full text].
- Where it breaks (from the same sources): (1) too low a level → audibility, not masking, limits performance (hence audibility checks and exclusions in PART, 4/40); (2) devices with a poor low-frequency response or narrow bandwidth change the effective masker spectrum (AirPods shifted tone-in-noise thresholds by ~3 volume steps in Zhao et al. 2022); (3) environmental noise adds an uncontrolled masker (outdoor vs anechoic thresholds +4.7 dB in Zhao et al.); (4) at very favourable SNRs a fixed-noise design must raise speech level, at very poor SNRs a fixed-speech design must raise noise, so fixed-overall-level designs (Koopmans et al.) or capped ranges (Mok et al. +6 dB cap) avoid clipping and loudness cues.

### 6d. Latency, timing and sample rate in browsers

- **Audio timing in browsers (Bridges, Pitiot, MacAskill & Peirce 2020, PeerJ 8:e9414, "timing mega-study")** [full text, PeerJ]: online audio onset cannot be hardware-verified; audio–visual asynchrony in browser packages ranged from about −44 to +286 ms in mean lag with trial-to-trial SDs 1–40 ms depending on package × OS × browser (worst: one package on macOS Safari, 286 ms lag, SD 26 ms); in some combinations sound failed to play reliably. Lab packages achieve <1 ms SD. Authors: browser JavaScript is not yet ready for precisely timed audio onsets. Implication for measures: response-time (effort) measures and any audio–visual sync are noisy across devices; within-device comparisons are safer than across-device ones.
- **Bluetooth**: phone-to-receiver latency averaged ~244–250 ms on the best Android phones tested and ~484 ms on another, with tens of ms run-to-run variation (SoundGuys/Android Authority 2019 bench test, 100 runs per codec, WALT tool) [full text, tech press]; aptX Low Latency rare/obsolete. Remote matrix test found no Bluetooth-vs-wired effect on SRT (Schwarz et al. 2026) [full text], so latency matters for timing/RT and audio–visual tasks, not for SNR thresholds.
- **Web Audio spec facts (W3C Web Audio API; MDN AudioContext options)** [full text of relevant sections]: the AudioContext runs at the output device's preferred sample rate unless one is requested (range 3,000–768,000 Hz; unsupported rates throw); `decodeAudioData` resamples decoded audio to the context's rate; `baseLatency` = processing latency to the audio subsystem; `outputLatency` = estimate from buffer hand-off to first sample at the output device, can change while running; `getOutputTimestamp()` relates context time to `performance.now()`; a context may start only after user activation (`resume()` stays pending otherwise); `latencyHint` "interactive" (default) / "balanced" / "playback" or seconds, not guaranteed. MDN names 44.1 kHz as the most common rate; 48 kHz is also widespread (my addition). Consequence (my inference): assets authored at one rate are resampled on devices running at the other, which matters little below ~20 kHz but can shift sharp band edges slightly.
- **Browser audio recording latencies differ by platform (Vogt, Hauber, Kuhlen & Abdel Rahman 2022, Behav Res Methods 54:1954–1975)** [full text, PMC; first 100k characters]: the same spoken-naming experiment gave latencies ~130 ms shorter on one survey platform than on jsPsych, attributed to the audio implementation; 31/48 participants on one platform showed recording-length variability in >20% of files vs 10/59 and 6/69 on jsPsych setups — voice-response timing is implementation-dependent.

### 6e. Safe listening

- **WHO–ITU safe listening standard (2019; ITU-T H.870, updated 03/2022)** [WHO publication page + ITU "Safe listening standard" PDF, full text]: weekly sound allowance — mode 1 (adults) 1.6 Pa²h per 7 days (= 80 dB(A) for 40 h); mode 2 (children/sensitive users) 0.51 Pa²h (= 75 dB(A) for 40 h); equal-energy rule (e.g. 100 dB for 16 min ≈ 80 dB for 40 h; dose doubles per +3 dB); rolling 7-day window; warnings at a preset level (e.g. 80%) and at 100%; daily share ≈ 1/7 (~15%); if a 100% warning is not acknowledged, output drops to the mode's level. Applies to headphone/earphone listening only; with unknown headphones the dosimeter assumes worst-case sensitivity; estimation uncertainty can be several dB (3 dB error = 100% dose error), so devices should not label low readings "safe". Arithmetic from the standard's definitions (mine): the mode-1 allowance equals ~400 h/week at 70 dB(A), ~12.6 h at 85 dB(A), ~4 h at 90 dB(A); a 20-min session is therefore ~0.1%, ~2.6% and ~8% of the weekly allowance at those levels. A browser app cannot measure dB SPL without calibration; published remote tests instead ask users to set a "comfortable" level from a low starting volume (Mok et al.: system volume 10–20% then adjust) and cap stimulus peaks relative to that reference.

## 7. Self-report and EMA

### 7a. Established questionnaires — psychometrics as summarised by NASEM 2025 [full text of Ch. 6, secondary for the primary studies]

- **APHAB** (Abbreviated Profile of Hearing Aid Benefit; Cox & Alexander 1995): 24 items, 4 subscales; "APHAB-global" = 18 items over the three communication subscales; ~10 min; ≥20 languages. 95% critical differences per subscale 26% (unaided/aided) and 33% (benefit); APHAB-global 17.8% (2-week retest) and 15.9% (10-week retest) (Chisolm et al. 2005). Chosen by NASEM as the strongest self-report candidate for speech understanding in complex situations.
- **SSQ** (Speech, Spatial and Qualities of Hearing scale; Gatehouse & Noble 2004): SSQ49 (49 items, three subscales), SSQ12 (12 items, single score), French SSQ15 (keeps subscales; less evidence). NASEM: good supplemental candidate when spatial hearing/sound quality matter.
- **HHIE family**: HHIE 25 items (~10 min; most evidence of sensitivity to change, incl. 3 RCTs); HHIE-S 10 items (2–3 min; much test–retest evidence); RHHI 18 items (72% shared with HHIE; reliability assumed from HHIE); RHHI-S 10 items. Psychosocial outcome, not listening ability per se.
- CPHI excluded by NASEM because of copyright restrictions (only explicit licensing statement found in that chapter).
- No MCIDs exist for RHHI, APHAB-global, QuickSIN, SSQ or WIN (NASEM).

### 7b-2. Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) in hearing research

- **Holube, von Gablenz & Bitzer 2020 (Ear Hear 41 Suppl 1)** [abstract only]: EMA = repeated in-the-moment ratings of individual everyday situations on smartphones, optionally paired with acoustic statistics (levels, averaged spectra — raw audio recording of bystanders is illegal in many countries). Open issues: unsupervised device handling; accuracy of description vs number of comparable situations; random vs target-oriented prompts; ability to respond during the task.
- **Schinkel-Bielefeld, Burke, Holube, Iankilevitch, Jenstad, Lelic, Naylor, Singh, Smeds, von Gablenz, Wolters & Wu 2024 (Am J Audiol 33:648–673), tutorial** [full text, Nottingham repository]: 3 weeks × 8 random prompts/day was too burdensome; 2 weeks × 6/day well accepted; informal analyses suggest 1 week × 7–8 surveys/day captures a person's auditory reality and a second week adds little; self-initiated surveys (e.g. 9 speaking situations/week over 4 weeks) are an alternative; keep each survey ≤3 min; timeouts 15–30 min. Response formats: labelled single choice, sliders, stars/emoji, context multiple choice, free text; paired comparisons with graded answers and a "cannot decide" option are most precise for A/B comparisons. Compliance falls with burden; employed people comply less; reactivity generally not a problem. Speech-in-noise situations tend to be under-reported relative to logged device data (quiet situations over-reported). Recommendations: short mandatory core for difficult situations (speech in noise, groups); cover weekdays and weekends; classify situations with the Common Sound Scenarios (CoSS) framework; screen careless responding (speed, straight-lining, inconsistency); analyse single cases with non-overlap of all pairs (NAP) or multilevel models; report with the CREMAS checklist.

- **What EMA misses (Am J Audiol 2020;29(3S):591–609, "What situations are we missing?")** [full text, ASHA; author names not shown on the page read — reported elsewhere as Schinkel-Bielefeld et al.]: 20 hearing-aid users, ~3 weeks; 8 random prompts/day (15-min window) + up to 4 "loud environment" prompts/day (level >65 dB SPL, ≥75 min apart) + ~5 self-initiated/day; mandatory short part (≤7 questions) + optional long part (≤20). 3,752 reports, 9.0/day; 71% of random-prompt answers within 1 min; app captured ~75% of wear time. Missed prompts: 46% inappropriate (impolite), 35% unsafe, 36% unheard. Speech-in-noise and car situations under-represented, quiet over-represented; immediately answered long questionnaires held only ~1/3 of the expected speech-in-noise situations, ~2/3 when loud-environment triggers were added; for some people up to 71% of speech-in-noise data missing. Social situations are where people stop reporting.
- **Response shift in retrospective ratings (Arthur, Watts, Davies, Manchaiah & Slater 2016, Audiol Res 6:152)** [full text, Swansea repository]: when hearing-aid users re-rated their *pre-fitting* GHABP disability retrospectively after 14 weeks, they rated it significantly worse than they had at baseline (n = 16, p = 0.00003) — baseline-vs-follow-up self-report can under- or over-state change because the internal standard shifts; GHABP repeat correlation >0.89 over 3 weeks (Gatehouse 1999, as cited).

### 7c. Situations to cover (from established instruments, paraphrased — for constructing original items, not for copying)

- SSQ12 situation themes [full text, Noble et al. 2013]: one talker with TV on; following two streams at once; one person among many talkers; group conversation in a busy restaurant; following turn-taking when the talker changes; locating sounds; judging distance and movement of vehicles; segregation of simultaneous sounds; identifying instruments; clarity of everyday sounds; concentration/effort needed to listen.
- GHABP fixed situations [full text, Whitmer et al. 2014]: TV with volume set for others; one-to-one in quiet; conversation on a busy street or in a shop; group conversation. Each situation is first screened for relevance (whether it occurs in the person's life) before rating.
- Situation taxonomy for original items: the EMA tutorial (Schinkel-Bielefeld et al. 2024) recommends classifying listening situations with the Common Sound Scenarios (CoSS) framework for comparability [full text of tutorial; CoSS paper itself not opened].

### 8b. Generic, published methods anyone can implement with original material

- **Digit triplets in speech-spectrum noise** with 1-up/1-down tracking (Smits et al. 2004 and >39 language versions reviewed by Van den Borre et al. 2021) — the method is published in detail (step sizes, scoring, level homogenisation) [full texts of several versions above]. Recordings and noise of any particular national version belong to their makers.
- **Matrix principle** (Hagerman 1982; Kollmeier et al. 2015): 5-slot, 10-alternative closed vocabulary, recombined sentences, per-word level homogenisation, word scoring, Brand & Kollmeier (2002) adaptive rule — published openly [Puglisi et al. 2015 full text]. The vocabulary lists and recordings of each language version are the developers'.
- **Coordinate-response style task** (CRM corpus, Bolia et al. 2000: a call sign plus a colour–number target in a fixed carrier sentence — carrier wording from memory, Bolia not opened) used in PART with a 4 colour × 8 number response grid and progressive TMR tracking [full text, PART 2020]; the task format is generic.
- **GRID corpus** (Cooke, Barker, Cunningham & Shao 2006): 34 talkers (18 m, 16 f) × 1,000 six-word matrix-style commands (e.g. "put red at G9 now"), 25 kHz audio + video; Zenodo record (2020 deposit) states CC BY 4.0, while the older Sheffield project page says "freely available for research use" [full text, both pages] — an openly licensed multi-talker closed-set corpus (attribution required; CC BY is not a non-commercial licence). Exact word-slot sizes not confirmed (JASA paper PDF did not render).
- **Adaptive rules** (Levitt 1971 transformed up-down — from memory, not opened; Plomp & Mimpen 1979 sentence SRT as cited by Lie et al. 2024 and Rosen et al. 2013; Brand & Kollmeier 2002 word-scored rule; UML/Bayesian methods, Shen et al.; BPACE 2023) — all published methods.
- **Headphone checks** (anti-phase, Huggins pitch, BMLD chirp) — published with stimulus specifications (Woods et al. 2017; Milne et al. 2021; Mok et al. 2024).
- **Words-in-noise with descending SNR + Spearman-Kärber scoring** — generic psychophysical scoring; the WIN word recordings are VA material.

## 9. Remote / online speech-in-noise testing, 2015–2026

- **Remote German matrix test on uncalibrated consumer devices (Schwarz, Schell-Majoor, Saak, Bruns & Kollmeier 2026, Trends Hear 30)** [full text, publisher page]: 20 young NH native speakers; one lab session plus two remote sessions ≥1 week apart in a browser-based "virtual hearing clinic" on their own devices (17/20 Android phones or tablets); adaptive 50% track with two training lists first. Remote test–retest r = 0.82, ICC(2,1) = 0.80, RMSE 0.73 dB, mean retest change −0.28 dB (n.s.). Remote vs lab: remote ~0.29 dB worse (n.s.), r ≈ 0.76–0.78, ICC 0.78, RMSE 0.74 dB, limits of agreement −1.07 to +1.65 dB. Between-subject SD 0.94 dB vs residual SD 0.50 dB. Headphone type (in-ear vs on/over-ear; wired vs Bluetooth) had no significant effect. Participants with earlier exposure to the test were ~1.5 dB better than naive ones (procedural/content learning that persists). Cites ~1 dB training effect over the first two lists and ~2 dB improvement over a year of repeated use. Session ~20 min. Limits: n=20, young NH only; some numbers inconsistent between abstract and results (r 0.78 vs 0.76; p 0.15 vs 0.08).
- **Web-based psychoacoustics with online screening (Mok, Viswanathan, Borjigin, Singh, Kafi … Bharadwaj 2024, Behav Res Methods 56:1433–1448)** [full text, publisher page; last part of the page truncated]: jsPsych-based tasks; online hearing screen = Modified Rhyme Test words (6-alternative closed set) in 4-talker babble at +10/+5/0/−5 dB SNR; pass = 100% at +10, ≥83% at +5, ≥75% at 0 dB, cut-offs chosen to exclude ~35% of the pool; also excluded anyone with >2 window "blur" events (left the tab) during the 6–7 min screen. Validation in 72 people with diagnosed hearing loss: ~19% still passed (sensitivity ~81% for any loss; 31% of mild and 3% of more-than-mild losses passed); specificity >65% for NH. Online vs lab effect sizes for F0 discrimination, gap detection, ITD, ILD near zero (Hedges g −0.14 to 0.06); consonant-confusion clusters reproduced lab data. Test–retest not reported; between-group effects not validated.
- **PART tablet battery, lab validation (Lelo de Larrea-Mancera, Stavropoulos, Hoover, Eddins, Gallun & Seitz 2020, JASA 148:1831–1851)** [full text, eScholarship PDF]: 150 young NH students, iPads, two headphone types; speech spatial release from masking (SRM) with CRM sentences, male target fixed 65 dB SPL, two male maskers co-located or at ±45°; progressive (not adaptive) track of 20 trials: masker rises 55→73 dB SPL in 2 dB steps every two trials; threshold TMR = 10 dB minus number correct; ~5 min per test. Test–retest (sessions ≤1 week apart): co-located bias 0.36 dB, 95% LoA −4.7 to 3.9 dB, r = 0.39; separated bias 1.12 dB (significant session effect, i.e. learning), LoA −6.7 to 4.5, r = 0.61; SRM bias 0.78, LoA −6.1 to 7.6, r = 0.47. Implied SD of differences ≈2.2 / 2.9 / 3.5 dB (my back-calculation from LoA). Low r partly from restricted range in young NH. Group means matched earlier lab data (co-located 1.94 dB, separated 4.47 dB, SRM 6.19 dB). 3-SD outliers were mostly inconsistent across sessions (inattention).
- **Online tone-in-noise psychophysics (Zhao et al. 2022)** [full text]: 3-down-1-up 40-trial tracks, mean threshold −19.5 dB SNR (SD 1.39); mean within-person range across three tracks 1.71 dB; first track less stable (practice); a single 20-trial track was "reasonably accurate"; fatigue correlated with poorer thresholds.
- **Online Arabic and English DIN at home vs lab (Shehabi, Plack, Prendergast, Munro, Stone, Laycock, AlJasser & Guest; accepted manuscript, Lancaster eprints 224745; journal/year not printed in the copy read)** [full text]: browser suite (MOSS) on own computers and headphones; subjective level calibration (a soft sentence must be clearly audible, a sentence 25 dB higher "loud but not uncomfortable"; test level 5 dB below the loud one); digits 1–9 without replacement after a carrier phrase, speech-shaped noise, 0.12–8 kHz band-pass, diotic; scored correct if ≥2/3 digits right; 2-down-1-up from +8 dB SNR, 6 dB steps for 2 turnpoints then 2 dB for 6; threshold = mean of last 6 turnpoints; ~26.5 trials, ~5 min; one practice block. Native Arabic speakers (n = 52): Arabic −10.75 dB (SD 0.92), English −11.49 dB (SD 1.10). Home vs lab Arabic: −10.23 vs −10.75 dB (0.52 dB, n.s. after correction); between-person SD larger at home (1.44 vs 0.92 dB). Mean absolute test–retest difference 1.12–1.18 dB (lab and home similar); ICCs very low (0.09–0.33) because NH between-person variance is tiny. Retest ~0.4 dB better (small learning despite practice and demos). Digits were not level-equalised (adds variance). Quoted prior values: telephone DIN SD of test–retest differences 1.71 dB, mean retest change 0.34 dB (Dillon et al. 2016); intra-individual SD <1 dB (Jansen et al. 2010); 0.7–1.3 dB with minor learning, and low-cost headphones within 1.5 dB of high-quality ones (Vlaming et al. 2014); training effect up to ~1.5 dB at retest (Smits et al. 2013, as cited); DIN slope 15–20 %/dB in SSN (Van den Borre et al. 2021, as cited); Arabic DIN −9.3 dB (SD 0.7) with broadband headphones but ~2 dB worse over band-limited telephone (Koifman et al. 2016, as cited).
- **ASR scoring of spoken DIN responses (Araiza-Illan, Meyer, Truong & Başkent; arXiv 2312.12269, c. 2023–24)** [full text, arXiv]: open-source Kaldi-NL scored spoken triplets with mean word error rate 5.0% (SD 8.8; one speaker 48%); errors were mostly spurious "1" insertions after noise; simulated SRT shifts from decoding errors 0.34–0.71 dB, i.e. comparable to the 0.70 dB within-subject SD of the DIN in young NH (Smits et al. 2013, as cited). Laptop loudspeakers lacked energy below ~630 Hz.
- **Smartphone DIN campaigns**: French antiphasic DIN app, 19,545 completed tests in one month, no calibration, any headphones (Ceccato et al. 2021) [full text]. South African smartphone DIN in clinics with self-set volume: r_s = 0.81 with PTA (Potgieter et al. 2018) [full text].
- **PART on participants' own devices (2022)** — see 6b: home vs lab composite test–retest r 0.67 vs 0.65; no calibrated-vs-uncalibrated effect; more outliers at home [full text].
- **Unsupervised ASR-scored matrix test project (DFG SAMT, 2018–2023)** — aims only, no results on the page read [full text, project page].

### 9b. What went wrong or added error in remote testing (collected from the sources above)

1. **People not on headphones / unsuitable equipment**: 35% of a large MTurk sample failed the anti-phase screen (Woods et al. 2017); ~40% of an unknown Prolific sample estimated not on suitable headphones (Milne et al. 2021); laptop speakers slip through the AP screen ~18% of the time; some listeners use surface vibration [full texts].
2. **Larger between-person spread at home** (online DIN SD 1.44 vs 0.92 dB lab) though mean shifts are small (0.3–0.5 dB) (Shehabi et al.; Schwarz et al. 2026) [full texts]; more ±3 SD outliers at home, mostly inconsistent across sessions, i.e. inattention (PART 2020/2022) [full texts].
3. **Attrition and disengagement**: 34% attrition in an online psychophysics study (Zhao et al. 2022); exclusion of participants who left the browser tab >2 times (Mok et al. 2024); fatigue predicted worse thresholds (Zhao et al.) [full texts].
4. **Environment**: background noise and distractions unmeasured (Shehabi et al.; PART 2022); outdoor vs anechoic +4.7 dB for tone-in-noise (Zhao et al.) [full texts].
5. **Device acoustics**: AirPods vs other headphones ~3 volume steps; laptop loudspeakers lacking energy below ~630 Hz; telephone band-limiting worsened Arabic DIN SRT by ~2 dB (Zhao et al.; Araiza-Illan et al.; Koifman et al. as cited) [full texts / secondary].
6. **Software/stimulus bugs**: programming/parameter errors removed tasks in PART 2022; audible seams in looped noise (Zhao et al.); non-equalised digit recordings inflated variance (Shehabi et al.); spurious "extra digit" heard at noise onset (Koopmans et al. 2018) [full texts].
7. **Timing**: audio–visual lag up to ~286 ms and jitter up to ~40 ms SD across browser/OS combinations; sound sometimes failed to play (Bridges et al. 2020); Bluetooth adds ~250–480 ms (tech press 2019) [full texts].
8. **Restricted range makes ICC/r look poor in young NH samples** (online DIN ICC 0.09–0.33; PART r 0.39–0.61) even when absolute agreement is ~1 dB — absolute indices (SD of differences, LoA, RMSE) are the informative ones for individual change [full texts].
9. **Screening by speech-in-noise performance online lets many mild losses through** (31% of mild losses passed in Mok et al. 2024) [full text].
